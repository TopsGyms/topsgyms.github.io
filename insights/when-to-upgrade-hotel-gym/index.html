When Should a Hotel Upgrade Its Gym?

A hotel should consider upgrading its gym when the current space no longer supports guest needs, property positioning, safe and reliable operation or planned business direction. The response should match the cause.

By Sofie Tops — Founder, TopsGyms18 September 2026
Answer in briefUpgrade signals include a weak guest experience, incomplete training offer, recurring operational issues, a mismatch with positioning and upcoming property investment. Diagnose before deciding the scale of change.

Look for patterns, not one isolated complaint

A single comment may identify a real issue, but an upgrade decision should consider the wider picture. Combine guest feedback, staff observations, equipment condition, use patterns, service history and the property’s future plans. Repeated friction across these sources is more informative than a request for one specific machine.

Ask what has changed. Guest expectations may have shifted, the hotel may be repositioning, equipment may have become unreliable or the room may no longer support the way people train. The trigger determines the right response.

Five signs that improvement deserves attention

The gym feels below the standard of the stay

When the rest of the property has evolved and the fitness space has not, the contrast becomes visible. This can affect confidence even when the equipment technically works. Review presentation, lighting, maintenance, clarity and the route into the space before assuming everything must be replaced.

Guests cannot complete relevant workouts

Missing strength options, poor balance, inadequate open space or an impractical layout can limit training quality. An inventory may look complete while the experience remains fragmented.

Operations are absorbing recurring problems

Frequent faults, difficult cleaning, poor storage, noise complaints or confusing access create ongoing cost and inconsistency. These are not only maintenance issues; they can indicate that the concept no longer fits the property.

The gym no longer supports positioning

A renewed wellness proposition, a move into a different market segment or a focus on active travel may require a more credible fitness offer. The upgrade should express the new direction rather than simply modernise finishes.

A wider renovation creates an opportunity

Planned building work may make it practical to address services, acoustics, access or room allocation that would otherwise remain fixed. Fitness strategy should be included early enough to influence these decisions.

Choose the right level of intervention

Not every signal requires a full refurbishment. A targeted improvement might reorganise zones, add missing training options, improve storage and clarify guidance. Selective replacement may address unreliable or unsuitable items. A broader upgrade becomes relevant when the layout, infrastructure and concept all prevent the gym from meeting its purpose.

Define the desired outcome before selecting the intervention. “Make it newer” is not enough. A clearer objective might be to enable a complete short workout, align the room with a wellness repositioning or reduce operational friction.

Plan timing around continuity

Consider procurement, installation, building work, guest communication and temporary alternatives. A staged approach can protect availability, but only if the final layout remains coherent. Avoid accumulating isolated purchases that make a future redesign harder.

Assess before committing

A structured review helps distinguish visible symptoms from the underlying problem. It should connect guest experience, training quality, equipment, space and flow, positioning and business potential. This creates priorities and makes it easier to explain why each investment is needed.

The right time to upgrade is when there is a clear gap between what the gym delivers and what the property needs it to deliver — and enough understanding to close that gap deliberately.

Build a decision case

Summarise the current gap, supporting evidence, desired outcome and likely level of intervention. Include the consequence of delay and dependencies with wider hotel work. This gives leadership a stronger basis than a collection of product proposals.

Distinguish essential action from opportunity. Reliability, safety and compliance questions require appropriate qualified review. Experience and positioning opportunities can then be prioritised alongside investment capacity and project timing.

Avoid the choice between nothing and everything

Many properties can phase improvement. An initial phase may resolve maintenance, layout and guidance. A second can add missing training options. Later refurbishment may address infrastructure or concept. Phasing works when every step contributes to the same direction.

Set review points after implementation. The goal is not constant change, but preventing the gym from becoming disconnected from the property until another urgent upgrade is required.
